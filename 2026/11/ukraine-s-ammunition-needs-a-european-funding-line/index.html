Ukraine's ammunition needs a European funding line

Congress votes the money, but one office decides whether it moves. Europe's governments budget as if that were guaranteed, and they should not.

6 November 2026

Ukraine's ammunition is financed by someone else's Congress, and that is not sovereignty, it is borrowed time. On Wednesday morning, the day after the Democrats took both houses of Congress, European defence stocks fell while the rest of the market rose: BAE Systems down 5.2 percent, Rheinmetall 3.2 percent, Hensoldt 4.9 percent, Thales 4.5 percent, Saab 3.8 percent. Investors spent a single morning pricing a fact our governments refuse to price: that the shells Ukraine fires depend on an appropriations vote in Washington that no European minister controls.

The Pentagon sat on $400 million in Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative military assistance for months this year, despite congressional authorisation. In August the Senate passed the Lindsey Graham Act with 86 votes, on 16 October the president waived its sanctions, and the Treasury had lifted restrictions on Russian diesel purchases days before. Every one of those events was a warning. Each one arrived between budget cycles, and each one was absorbed as if it were weather. A new majority does not change that arithmetic. James M. Lindsay of the Council on Foreign Relations notes that a Democratic Congress may bristle at the president's diplomacy with Russia but cannot compel him to change it, and most lawmakers hesitate to oppose a president of their own party. So 86 votes bought a law that was waived by 16 October. We are told a friendlier Congress is the fix. The record says Congress was never the problem.

A Senate majority of 54 seats, with two races still open, moves the money question to a friendlier committee. It does not move the signature. The president spent the morning after the count telling CBS News he believed Democrats would want to work with him, while posting a picture of a red veto cap to show what happens if they do not. Mike Levin, a Democratic representative for California's 49th district, says Democrats will use every legislative and oversight tool available to ensure Ukraine receives the security assistance it needs, and we take him at his word. A veto, a waiver or a withheld signature can stop a line overnight, and no European parliament gets a vote on any of them.

That is the case for waiting, and it collapses on the process it trusts. James M. Lindsay of the Council on Foreign Relations argues that even a Democratic Congress cannot compel this president to change his diplomacy with Russia, and that most lawmakers hesitate to cross a president of their own party. A majority votes the money; the signature that moves it still sits in one office, and a later Congress can undo November in a single night.

The Council should stop waiting for a friendly committee chair. At its next meeting it should agree a multi-year funding instrument, written into Union law and paid from the Union budget, covering the conventional military assistance Ukraine now receives or expects under American programmes, sized against the authorisations and delays set out above. The European Commission should table the draft within three months. Whoever holds the Council presidency in January owns this piece of work, and we will count what it delivers.
