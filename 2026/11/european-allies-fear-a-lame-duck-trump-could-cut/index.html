European allies fear a lame-duck Trump could cut US troops in Europe, diplomats say

The Pentagon has already cancelled a brigade rotation to Poland and announced up to 5,000 troop cuts, while Congress set a floor of 76,000 US forces in Europe.

3 November 2026

Five NATO diplomats said European allies fear that a Republican defeat in the US midterm elections could push Donald Trump to cut US military support for Europe. Three of them told Politico that a lame-duck Trump might pull thousands more troops out of Europe to cement his foreign policy legacy, using the Pentagon's ongoing review to do it.

A senior European defence official said lame-duck presidents typically vent frustrations and engage in foreign policy adventurism, and that attacking NATO seems a consistent part of the Trump worldview. NATO diplomats said he could cut US contributions to NATO common funding and limit PURL, the scheme that lets Europe buy US arms for Ukraine. NATO diplomats said he could also deepen ties with Russia. The diplomats told Politico he might tie troop deployments, Ukraine and European security to a broader agreement with Putin.

Colby, the US defence official running the Pentagon review of the US military presence in Europe, said it is meant to move Europe fast and irreversibly toward primary responsibility for its own conventional defence. The Pentagon has cancelled an armoured brigade rotation to Poland and announced up to 5,000 troop cuts, mainly in Germany. Congress has set a floor: the 2026 defence bill forbids cutting US forces in Europe below 76,000 without first consulting allies. The House version passed in July would extend that floor and order a review of the 5,000-troop cut.

The Czech Republic fulfils only 30 percent of NATO capability building targets and meets another 70 insufficiently, with the hardest ones lagging five to ten years behind. That deficit limits the army's ability to carry out collective defence tasks, the Czech assessment states. Its own concept document says the army could not defend the state's territory, guard non-military objects important for defence, or adequately support allies in a conflict.

Czech defence spending could reach two percent of GDP for the first time next year, the minimum the alliance set in 2014. The Babiš government plans no increase for 2028 and 2029, the reported figures state. It aims for 2.25 percent only at the end of that period. The army concept says that at this level real development of forces is not expected. Moscow said around 29 October that it is prepared for a potential conflict with Europe.
