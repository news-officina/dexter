Water cannon answers the riot. Only a budget answers the protest.

France and Germany met a generation's demands with 70,000 police and prosecutors' circulars, and nothing else. A state that can only repress, not respond, will keep repressing.

2 November 2026

A state that can only repress, not respond, will keep repressing. That is the lesson of this weekend, when young people marched in France and Germany, and neither Paris nor Berlin has learned it. The French government's answer was 70,000 police and gendarmes, elite units among them, with Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez warning beforehand that the day looked like "a very high risk".

The numbers tell one story. A molotov cocktail was thrown at a mobile gendarme in Paris; Nuñez called it attempted murder, and noted the gendarme did not stop his service. Back on 9 October, Justice Minister Darmanin had already convened the 36 procureurs généraux twice to give them clear instructions during the lycéen movement. The machinery of prosecution works, and we do not ask it to stop. A court for everyone who throws a firebomb.

But count what else marched. In Germany, the youth movement Gen Z regelt says some 120,000 people took part, below the around 180,000 expected; its spokesman, giving only his first name, Amadeo, said more than 100,000 had done so. Read the movement's own demands: more money for schools, addiction help and psychotherapy, measures against rising living costs, social justice and more climate protection. It rejects conscription and expanded state surveillance. The movement explicitly disowned the violence, saying extremist groups, anti-constitutional symbols, racism and hate speech have "no place with us, no matter from which side". These are not rioters. They are a generation handing over its bill and asking to see the other side of the ledger. The bill for decline has been made the business of the riot police alone.

The strongest case for the governments deserves its answer. Yielding to the street rewards violence, and the attempted murder in Paris is exactly that; concede nothing while stones fly. On the criminals, we agree, and the law should take its course. But for the country, the argument fails. Nobody marches for a riot. Crush the violence, and the queue for the next demonstration forms anyway, this time in front of recruiters who do have an answer for the young, just not one that keeps a Europe worth living in.

So here is what must be done, and it belongs to the two governments. Put cost-of-living relief and public services into the next budgets as explicit, funded measures: energy prices, housing, school upkeep. Set them next to what this same generation is asking for, so the young can see what each euro buys. Policing continues under the law. But no further weekend passes where the only public answer to this protest is a number of officers. Water cannon answers the riot. Only a budget answers the protest.
