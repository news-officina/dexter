A sanction Russia can shop around is not a sanction, it is a leak

The Court of Auditors found that enforcement varies by capital and companies pick the weakest border. The EU pays the full cost of the embargo and delivers only part of the pressure, so enforcement must move to Brussels.

11 November 2026

Twenty-one packages of sanctions, and a Commission that still will not touch enforcement. That is the finding of the Court of Auditors, published on 10 November: rules that read differently in each capital, checks that vary from one customs post to the next, and a Brussels executive that calls the whole thing a national responsibility. A sanction Europe cannot apply the same way twice is a weapon Russia can shop around. We argue for one European enforcement regime, decided where the sanctions are decided, because pressure scattered across national borders is pressure lost.

The gap is documented, not a theory. The auditors found member states struggling with weak monitoring, spotty enforcement and legal ambiguity, and named what differs: how many authorities handle sanctions, how infringements are prosecuted. The Commission is the authority charged with monitoring that sanctions are applied harmoniously across the union, and its answer to the auditors was that member states are responsible and it has no mandate to intervene. And the auditors are explicit about what that produces: companies deliberately choosing licensing offices and customs posts where the checks are weakest. They call it forum shopping.

The trade routes through those holes already exist. Birch plywood from Russia and Belarus, relabelled in a foreign state, ends up on the EU market the sanctions were meant to close. Kazakhstan, which traded almost no ball bearings with us before the war, imported €58 million of them from the EU in 2025 and shipped €34 million of them on to Russia and Belarus. New bypass hubs are forming in Southeast Asia, in Malaysia and Thailand. This is the clock we are against: every month a loophole stays open, a bypass business in a third country becomes a permanent industry, and the smugglers learn faster than our enforcement adapts. The weapon is leaking while we debate who holds it.

Those who would keep enforcement national have a number: restrictions on exports cost €49 billion, bans on imports €110 billion, at 56 and 75 percent of pre-war levels, so why add a central enforcer that only costs more? Because the auditors who dug into those rules say unclear wording and uneven checks cut the sanctions' impact and make them easier to circumvent. We pay the full price of the embargo in foregone trade and receive only part of the weapon. The costs come off Europe's ledger in full. Only what gets through the holes we have mapped reaches Moscow.

The record shows the weapon fires when it is aimed. Exports of war goods from EU countries have almost completely come to a standstill since 2023, after twenty-one packages. But the sanctions commissioner Maria Luís Albuquerque calls that working. Attach uniform enforcement and the auditors' two recommendations to the 22nd package, the one governments already want for their credibility on Ukraine, so that blocking it means explaining to voters why their border keeps the loophole. The Commissioners responsible must put uniform enforcement in that package, and the member states must adopt it in Brussels this month.
