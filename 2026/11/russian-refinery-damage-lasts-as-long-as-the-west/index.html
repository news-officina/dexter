Russian refinery damage lasts as long as the West keeps the repair parts banned

The strikes have taken half of Russia's refining capacity offline, yet exports rose to 7.7 million barrels a day. What decides whether the damage holds is not the number of drones but the export rules on secondary processing equipment, and those are held in Brussels and Washington.

1 November 2026

A drone battalion commander with the call sign Tango told the Washington Post that Ukrainian forces target the most important parts of refineries.

He serves in Ukraine's 1st Separate Center of Unmanned Systems. The aim, he said, is that they stop and do not recover.

It is a doctrine of lasting damage, stated by the man flying the drones. The IZA study found that 22 of 29 large Russian refineries sustained verified direct hits between June 2022 and May 2026. Frontelligence judged in an assessment published in March 2025 that in at least 67 of 100 Ukrainian strike cases the strikes were successful. In 33 cases, it found, the result is unknown or Ukraine's involvement cannot be proven.

Whether a hit means a stop that does not recover is the question this piece tests.

The question is whether the damage to Russia's refining capacity lasts. The answer matters now for how long Moscow keeps its fuel supply, and for whoever holds the rules on repair parts.

The stakes are measurable. OIES estimated net crude processing disruptions at 380 kb/d in March 2024, 12% of capacity in the targeted area and 6% of total capacity. Russian capacity otherwise exceeds domestic demand: OSW, citing Argus data, reported in August 2025 that about 44 million tonnes were produced in 2023 against 35 to 36 million tonnes consumed. Spare capacity, if parts arrive, buys time.

Where the strikes landed

The satellite record answers part of that. More than one-third of 54 analysed strikes appear to have hit secondary processing units, per the Washington Post. These refine distilled materials into gasoline, jet fuel and liquefied petroleum gas. Its geospatial analysis expert confirmed damage was evident in 57 strikes on Russian refineries between April 1 and August 31, 2026.

EU and US sanctions have banned export of distillation and secondary processing equipment to Russia since February 2022, per the Washington Post. Struck units are therefore hard to repair. Repeated strikes stress metal structures, making catastrophic failure more likely.

Frontelligence reported in March 2025 that the greatest damage fell on high-octane gasoline production. Catalytic cracking units produced by UOP or BASF have no analogues in Russia or China. Industry experts said seamless castings cannot be replaced under sanctions.

Carnegie argued that surplus distilling capacity absorbs hits on primary units. Destroying one of two identical distillation columns would more likely cut gasoline production by about 30%, not 50%. The secondary units, where the parts cannot be bought, remain the bottleneck.

The rhythm of the campaign

According to Tochnyi's data, cited by the Washington Post, mass strikes began in earnest in August of the prior year. They eased over the winter during negotiations, then accelerated from April 2026 and reached a new high this August.

OSW estimated in August 2025 that the summer wave had disabled more than 10% of Russia's refining capacity. The Washington Post, for its part, counted more than twice the late 2025 level offline by the end of August 2026.

That count is contested. Battlepolicy reported that Fire Point put capacity offline at about 14% after the Moscow strike, against the General Staff's claim of more than 45%. Battlepolicy judged the higher figure most plausibly counts every unit hit during the campaign, including repaired ones.

OPEC recorded refining down more than 23% quarter-on-quarter in Q3 2026, to 3.95 million barrels per day. Reuters calculations found three of what it counted as Russia's six largest diesel refineries cut or halted output in September. The Kyiv Post reported gasoline covered only about 70% of domestic demand, with at least 17 regions restricting fuel sales.

Energy Intelligence reported jet fuel output rose 5.6% in 2025 despite the outages.

Cases against lasting damage

The Carnegie Endowment argued in June 2024 that damaged units at many refineries had been back in operation after two to three weeks of repairs. It found refining volumes were down from their peaks but still within the customary band and above their troughs.

The KSE Institute recorded that Black Sea crude exports in June 2026 reached their highest level since the invasion.

On this reading, the strikes disrupt units, and Russia repairs them, reroutes the barrels and keeps the flows moving. The campaign, on these figures, delays rather than diminishes.

Domestic pain, export gains

Domestic damage is real: premium AI-98 gasoline cost almost 84 rubles per liter by late February 2025, which Milov called indirect confirmation of damage to high-octane fuel. Exports tell the opposite story.

The KSE Institute estimates total Russian crude and product exports rose by 390 kb/d to 7.7 mb/d, higher crude volumes only partly offset by lower product shipments.

IZA found that applying the same satellite method to 106 non-refinery deep-strike targets produces no comparable decline, which weighs against reading it as general war damage.

So what lasts is decided where the parts are banned, in Brussels and Washington. Struck secondary units have no maker in Russia or China, and the export rules that keep replacement equipment out are held there. The rules decide whether a struck refinery recovers.

The dispute over the figures cannot be settled from outside for now.

That leaves the next word to the external readings by Frontelligence, as they appear.
