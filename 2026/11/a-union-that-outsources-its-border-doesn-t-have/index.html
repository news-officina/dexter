A union that outsources its border doesn't have one

The Council majority calls a bigger Frontex unnecessary while returns still depend on deals that can collapse with one election abroad. Build the 30,000-strong corps properly, write the safeguards in, and stop pretending the border works.

2 November 2026

A union that outsources its border doesn't have one. On 27 October the Commission proposed a new Frontex Regulation: a standing corps of 30,000 officers by 2041, roughly triple today's force, with a 17,000-strong border and return reserve, a Quick Reaction Force and a Return Coordination Centre. It goes now to the Parliament and the Council. And there it meets a wall. In February, a Council summary showed a majority of member states do not perceive, at this stage, an added value or operational need for any significant enlargement of Frontex. Not that the plan needs fixing. That the border, as it stands, is someone else's job.

Look at what that majority is defending. Returns depend on deals that can collapse with one election abroad. The same Council note that questions growth admits the point against itself: any growth of the Standing Corps beyond the 2019 Regulation requires a discussion on Frontex's future mandate and operations. And then they refuse the only instrument that matches them. Poland stands apart: its deputy interior minister, Maciej Duszczyk, supports expanding Frontex's competences, even while its own Belarus border needs no more support. One member state sees the logic.

Now set the speech against the budget. Von der Leyen said in October that Europe proposes to strengthen Frontex so it can support member states whenever and wherever needed, including on returns. The budget draft for 2028 to 2034 already allocates 11.8 billion euros to Frontex, against almost 6 billion in the current perspective. The new Return Regulation entered into force on 16 October and it needs an enforcement arm to mean anything; a return rate above 30 percent is a start, not a system. The next generation will inherit the contradiction, and the bill for it.

The objection deserves its answer. Crossings have more than halved in two years. That number is the objection's best card. Because the problem is cyclical and the instrument must not be. A pressure that halves today can double again tomorrow, and the corps set for 2041 cannot be stood up in a crisis week. As for oversight: in September 2025, reports showed Greece with 12 Serious Incident Reports under review by the Fundamental Rights Office, covering alleged forced collective removals from 2023 to 2025. That record is real. It is an argument for writing safeguards into law, non-refoulement, independent fundamental-rights monitoring, transparency over third-country operations, not for a weaker force.

So the Council should do its job. Negotiate the proposal on its merits, not dismiss it. Fix the corps the 11.8 billion is already pledged to, with dated milestones, and give the agency the returns mandate the new Return Regulation needs, with the safeguards written firmly in. A union that outsources its border doesn't have one. Build the border. Own it.
