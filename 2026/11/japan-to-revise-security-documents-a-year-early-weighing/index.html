Japan to revise security documents a year early, weighing 50 to 60 trillion yen plan

The revision expands defence spending from fiscal 2027, below the Defense Ministry's own estimate, with Beijing objecting in advance to any renewed naming of China as Japan's chief challenge.

13 November 2026, updated 14 November 2026

Correction: An earlier version gave 17 December without its year; it was 2025.

Japan will revise its three security documents a year ahead of schedule, expanding defence spending for fiscal 2027 onward. The government cites Russia's invasion of Ukraine and China's maritime advances. Nikkei reports that the finance and defence ministries have begun coordinating defence spending for the five years from fiscal 2027 at 50 to 60 trillion yen.

The current plan, adopted at the end of fiscal 2022, raised five-year spending through fiscal 2026 from 27.5 trillion yen to 43 trillion yen. Long-range missiles for counterattack capability, equipment parts and ammunition sit at its centre. Nikkei reports the Defense Ministry had estimated the five-year total at 70 to 80 trillion yen.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told a press conference on 17 December 2025 that, on the defence tax increase, the government aims for a settlement that does not create new household burdens.

Washington is asking allies to raise defence spending to 3.5 percent of GDP. Reports say the revised documents would again name China as Japan's greatest strategic challenge. Beijing opposes this. China's Defence Ministry spokesperson Chen Xi said Japan's moves had betrayed its commitment to peace. They blatantly challenged the postwar international order and the authority of the Non-Proliferation Treaty.

Chen Xi said Japan's right-wing forces fabricate the China threat, stage themselves as victims, build long-range strike capability and attempt a gradual nuclear loosening. He called on the international community to stay vigilant and to uphold the nuclear non-proliferation regime against Japan's push for remilitarization. Tokyo's answer on record: Ambassador Tomiko Ichikawa said in April that Japan's three nonnuclear principles bar introducing nuclear weapons.
