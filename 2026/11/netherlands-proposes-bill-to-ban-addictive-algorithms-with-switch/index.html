Netherlands proposes bill to ban addictive algorithms, with switch-off as penalty

The PvdA proposal covers social media, games, dating apps and chatbots, and eight parliaments back it. Brussels' own cases against TikTok and Meta remain unfinished.

2 November 2026

On 2 November 2026, Barbara Kathmann, an MP for PvdA, the Dutch social-democratic party, introduced a Dutch bill to ban harmful, addictive algorithms. It defines manipulative design, such as endless scrolling and luring users into unplanned purchases, as banned unfair commercial practices.

Brussels has opened two preliminary cases against addictive design, and both are still unfinished. In February 2026 the Commission preliminarily found TikTok in breach of the Digital Services Act for addictive design, including infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and its highly personalised recommender system. The Commission followed in July 2026 with preliminary findings that Meta built Facebook and Instagram to be addictive, before a possible fine of up to 6% of global annual revenue.

The Dutch bill goes beyond social media. Kathmann's proposal also covers games, dating apps and chatbots. Her enforcement plan works in three steps: a warning first, then a hefty fine, and on a second offence the platform is switched off. According to a 14 September report by ORF, Austria's public broadcaster, countries wanting their own bans depend on the Commission, because only it can impose obligations on companies.

In Kathmann's words, these are designs that make people addicted and lure them into spending money. Kathmann also claims good laws proposed in Brussels are often lobbied away, and that there are more tech lobbyists than Members of the European Parliament. She points to France's proposal for a minimum age on social media as the precedent to follow.

Cross-party backing

Kathmann is not working alone. With her stand parliamentarians in Germany, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Austria, Italy and Portugal. They want the same rules written into law, so that they can no longer be lobbied away in Brussels. Her own words, in translation: a member state should not shy away from wanting to run ahead.

The Commission's position came from Henna Virkkunen, the tech chief. In July 2026, she said protecting Europeans' physical and mental health must be a priority for social media platforms. She stated that the Commission is fully committed to enforcing European law.

The platforms reject the charges. In February, TikTok called the Commission's depiction categorically false and entirely meritless, and said it would challenge the findings through every means available. Meta made the same argument in July. The findings, it said, do not take sufficient account of the significant measures it has taken to protect teenagers. It pointed to Teen Accounts, launched on Instagram in 2024.

The French precedent

The Dutch proposal follows a French attempt that failed. France first tried to ban named platforms, but the Constitutional Council struck the law down as a disproportionate interference with freedom of opinion and communication, as ORF reported in September. The revised French proposal therefore listed criteria instead: addictive algorithms prolonging children's screen time, automatic video playback, around-the-clock notifications and the ability to communicate with strangers. In an October 2025 publication by Rijksoverheid, the Dutch government website, signatories of the Jutland Declaration called for strict enforcement of existing rules like the DSA. They also called for obliging companies to take sufficient measures to protect children.

On 17 September 2026 the Commission issued the EU KIDS Act proposal, limiting autonomous account creation by children below 15 and harmonising age rules across the union. The Digital Fairness Act is expected on 11 November 2026 and will be negotiated in parallel. In July the co-chairs of the special panel appointed to examine children's use of digital services recommended an EU-wide access restriction, harmonised safety-by-design rules and privacy-preserving age assurance.
